\begin{abstract}
A self-improving agent system accepts a candidate variant through an acceptance
rule, and the rule's name is the interface every downstream consumer reads. We
audit one such rule: a function named \code{mcnemar\_exact} that gates
self-evolution promotion via
\code{promote = bool(new\_wins > old\_wins and p < 0.05)}. Its docstring admits
the body's reality (exact below $n{=}25$, normal approximation otherwise), but
the name advertises a rigour the body does not honour. Recomputing the entire
fallback region ($n{=}26\ldots500$, $b\ge c$: 62{,}819 cells, of which the gate
would promote 55{,}469) against the level-matched exact reference
(\code{scipy.stats.binomtest}, two-sided) yields 231 decision flips at
$\alpha{=}0.05$, and every one favors promotion \emph{relative to that
reference}. The fallback is an uncorrected normal approximation; the one-line
continuity correction replaces those 231 promotion-side flips with 9 of the
opposite sign, on a disjoint set of cells, while mid-p leaves 221
anti-conservative against the same exact reference: the fix is a dial, not a
restore. The largest absolute error is $0.152610$ at $b{=}14$, $c{=}13$, a cell
where the two-sided exact reference caps at $1.0$ (partly definitional); away
from the cap it is $0.150132$ at $b{=}14$, $c{=}12$. In the discontinuity band
the code returns $p=0.049860$ where the reference returns $0.075519$
($1.51\times$). A workspace-wide classifier finds 118 name-matched function
entries, 82 of them paired-binomial tests; the defect is localised to two copies
of one function, and a rejudgment of the system's fourteen-entry promotion
ledger shows no recorded decision actually flips. A public-code survey of 68
\code{exact}-named implementations finds one that breaks the same contract above
a sample-size threshold; the other 67 were not observed to do so. We report two
defects in our own tools, delimit what the audit does not establish, and state
the design space the audited rule sits in.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Self-improving agent systems accumulate candidates --- patched prompts, edited
skill banks, re-written code --- and must decide, for each one, whether it
replaces the incumbent. That decision is made by an \emph{acceptance rule}.
Unlike a benchmark score, an acceptance rule is consulted by every later
consumer of the system's history: whoever reads the promoted ledger inherits
whatever the rule actually did, not whatever its name suggested.

This paper audits one acceptance rule from a production self-evolution harness
in our workspace. The function is named \code{mcnemar\_exact}; it gates
promotion with

\begin{verbatim}
promote = bool(new_wins > old_wins and p < 0.05)
\end{verbatim}

Its docstring reads (translated from the original Chinese) ``two-sided exact
$p$; exact below $n{=}25$, normal approximation otherwise.'' So the body
is not hidden: a reader who opens the file learns the truth. But the name is
what the call site advertises, and the name claims a rigour the body does not
honour. That is this paper's subject in miniature: \textbf{an acceptance rule
that makes a rigour claim its body does not honour} --- and, as we will show
against the level-matched exact reference, the resulting error is not
symmetric noise. It is a one-sided bias in favour of promotion.

The question has practical teeth, though we draw the bridge carefully. The
\emph{pattern} --- promotion gates consulted once per candidate inside a
self-improvement loop --- is documented in the published literature:
\citep{he2026failure} analyzes 145 modifications eligible for analysis in
the public Darwin Gödel Machine logs --- six published runs, pooled ---
small-$n$ territory exactly where an exact test
and an uncorrected approximation disagree. That log is not this workspace and
that rule is not this function; the citation establishes regime similarity,
not shared history. And the same audit, run workspace-wide, shows the defect
is not widespread \emph{there}: it is two copies of one function, distinguished
from 118 neighbouring implementations by precisely the thing the audit is
built to surface --- a disagreement between what a function is called and what
it does. Public code supplies one further instance of the same defect
(\S\ref{sec:f02}), which is what moves the finding from ``two copies in one
workspace'' to ``a class of error with at least two independent occurrences''.

Our contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Measured one-sidedness, benchmark stated} (\S\ref{sec:f01}):
  recomputation of the entire fallback region against the level-matched
  two-sided exact reference --- 231/55{,}469 decision flips, all favoring
  promotion relative to \emph{that} reference. The qualification is load-bearing:
  against a one-sided $\alpha{=}0.05$ exact reference the same region reads
  928 conservative cells and none anti-conservative, so the direction claim is
  reference-relative and we say so.
  \item \textbf{Mechanism and design space} (\S\ref{sec:f01}): the fallback is
  an uncorrected normal approximation; the continuity correction replaces the
  231 promotion-side flips with 9 of the opposite sign, on a disjoint set of
  cells, while mid-p
  leaves 221 anti-conservative against the same reference, and the exact
  conditional test the audit treats
  as reference is itself known-conservative \citep{fagerland2013mcnemar} ---
  so switching to it trades bias for throughput rather than restoring truth.
  \item \textbf{Pattern or accident} (\S\ref{sec:f02}): a static classifier
  over 118 name-matched function entries, finding the defect localised to two
distinct
  copies of one function --- the only three hybrids, and all three named
  \code{mcnemar\_exact} --- with the classifier's name-gated recall stated as
  a boundary, not buried.
  \item \textbf{Dynamic validation and self-audit} (\S\ref{sec:dynamic},
  \S\ref{sec:selfaudit}): seven targets executed against the reference,
  including one whose largest error comes from a degenerate-tie artifact of
  its own correction term; two defects in our own tools, of the same class the
  audit hunts.
  \item \textbf{Ledger rejudgment} (\S\ref{sec:limits}): the audited system's
  fourteen recorded gate decisions, re-evaluated read-only against the exact
  reference --- all fourteen agree; the harm this paper measures is potential,
  and we show it.
  \item \textbf{Our own instruments, described correctly}
  (\S\ref{sec:semantics}): the audit CLI exits $0$ on a target it has just
  declared non-exact, so we call it a \emph{reporter} rather than a gate and
  scope the fail-closed claim to the selftest injection suite. We also report
  a correction to our own public-survey result: the first criterion asked
  whether an exact formula was present, and cleared 68 of 68; re-adjudicating
  for a sample-size switch finds one counterexample --- an
  \code{exact}-named function in \code{Gurp-S/XEYO} that returns a normal
  approximation above $n{=}500$. The corrected rate is $1/68$, reported as a
  floor and not as an ecosystem prevalence.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The gate under audit}\label{sec:gate}

The audited function lives at \code{evo\_agent/frozen\_v6/evolve.py:12}. Its
promotion line (line 44 of the file) is

\begin{verbatim}
promote = bool(new_wins > old_wins and p < 0.05)
\end{verbatim}

with $p$ from \code{mcnemar\_exact}. The McNemar test \citep{mcnemar1947} is the
right instrument for paired binary outcomes --- new-variant win/loss against
the incumbent on the same tasks --- so the choice of test is not the defect.
The defect is the switch: below $n{=}25$ discordant pairs the function
computes the exact conditional binomial tail; at $n \ge 26$ it silently
switches to a normal approximation. Four statements of the same contract
disagree:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Layer & Statement & Who sees it \\
\midrule
Name & \code{mcnemar\_exact} & every reader of the call site \\
Docstring & ``exact below $n{=}25$'' & readers who open the file \\
Boundary & docstring says $n{<}25$; body runs exact at $n{=}25$ too & readers who diff the two \\
Body & normal approximation at $n\ge 26$ & nobody, unless they test \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent A note on orientation, since the counts carry sign: the function's
docstring defines $b$ as old-wins/new-losses and $c$ as old-losses/new-wins,
and its call site passes \code{(old\_wins, new\_wins)} --- so the promotion
side is the call site's second argument. The $p$-value is symmetric in
$(b,c)$; the scans below state the promotion side as $b \ge c$, and by mirror
symmetry the two readings cover the same cells.

A name is the cheapest thing to trust and the most expensive thing
to be wrong about, because everything downstream of the call site trusts it by
default --- a contract lens with a thirty-year genealogy \citep{meyer1992contract}.

\section{F-01: the approximation is one-sided --- relative to its reference}\label{sec:f01}

\code{mcnemar\_audit.py} recomputes every cell of the fallback region against
an \emph{independent} reference: \code{scipy.stats.binomtest}
\citep{scipy2020}, the exact conditional binomial test (two-sided, default
exact method). The region is the gate's own decision surface, scanned over
$n = 26\ldots500$ with $b \ge c$ (the gate is directional; see the
orientation note in \S\ref{sec:gate}):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
Region $n{=}26\ldots500$, $b\ge c$ & 62,819 cells \\
Cells the gate would promote & 55,469 \\
Decision flips at $\alpha=0.05$ & 231 \quad (0.42\% of promotions at this bound) \\
Decision flips at $n \le 200$ & 84 \quad (1.02\% of its 8,204 promotions) \\
All 231 anti-conservative (vs.\ two-sided exact) & \textbf{True} \\
Largest absolute error & 0.152610 \quad at $b{=}14$, $c{=}13$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The one-sidedness is the finding, stated at the level it holds. At the
\emph{decision} level, against the two-sided exact reference, there is no
cell in the audited region where the approximation is stricter: all 231 flips
point the same way, the code promotes where the exact test holds back. The direction is a deterministic consequence of the missing correction, not a
random sign, and we report the count rather
than a significance test. The 231 cells are an enumeration of one formula's
boundary under a single reference, not independent trials, so a binomial tail
on them has no null to condition on. A reviewer of a promotion ledger produced
by this gate inherits a systematic bias, not a random one. Ties and near-ties
are outside the count from the start: 475 cells in the region carry an exact
two-sided $p$ of exactly $1.0$, so neither test can promote there and none of
them contributes a flip. At the \emph{p-value} level the claim would be false
--- in 41{,}076 of the 62{,}819 cells the approximation's $p$ is \emph{above}
the reference's (all deep-tail cells where both tests reject; the largest
code $p$ among them is $5.3\times10^{-5}$, far from any threshold) --- which is
exactly why the paper's claims are stated in decisions, not numbers.

The direction claim is also reference-relative, and the reference must be
named. The gate's promote condition applies one direction of a two-sided
test, and its $\alpha{=}0.05$ threshold doubles the one-sided tail --- this
is the \emph{level-matched} comparator, the one whose rejection region the
threshold was calibrated to, and against it the flips read anti-conservative.
Against a one-sided $\alpha{=}0.05$ exact reference the same region reads the
other way: 928 conservative cells, zero anti-conservative (exemplar: at
$b{=}18$, $c{=}8$, the code returns $0.049860$ against a one-sided exact
$0.037759$). Which comparator is ``right'' depends on what the caller means
by $p<0.05$; what is not defensible is reporting a direction without naming
the reference. We name it: two-sided, level-matched.

One qualification belongs to the comparison itself, and we state it rather
than let a reader supply it. The exact conditional test is conservative
\citep{fagerland2013mcnemar}, so at a common nominal $\alpha$ its achieved size
is below $0.05$; part of the 231-cell gap is therefore the reference's
conservatism rather than the approximation's missing correction, and these
data do not separate the two. The mid-p arm above is the nearest available
check: it removes much of the exact test's conservatism and still leaves 221
of the 231 cells anti-conservative, so the direction is not an artefact of the
reference's size alone. What a full separation would require is an
actual-size-matched comparison --- a randomized exact test, or an exact level
calibrated to the approximation's achieved size --- and that is a different
measurement from the one this audit makes.

The mechanism is textbook, and naming it sharpens the finding. The audited
fallback computes $z = |b-c|/\sqrt{b+c}$ and $p = 2\,Q(z)$ --- an uncorrected
normal approximation, whose missing $\pm\tfrac12$ continuity term is the
classical cause of exactly this signature: $p$ pulled below the reference
uniformly near the center, where the correction matters most. The correction
is a one-line change, and the two standard repairs sit on opposite sides:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrl}
\toprule
Variant & anti-cons.\ flips & cons.\ flips & max abs err \\
\midrule
audited (uncorrected normal) & 231 & 0 & 0.152610 \; $(14,13,27)$ \\
continuity-corrected $\chi^2$ & 0 & 9 & 0.002053 \; $(19,8,27)$ \\
exact, mid-p & 221 & 0 & 0.149446 \; $(14,13,27)$ \\
exact conditional (reference) & --- & --- & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The continuity correction replaces the promotion-side bias with a
conservative one on a disjoint set of cells; mid-p fixes only ten
of the 231 flips and leaves 221, each still anti-conservative against the same
exact reference; and the exact conditional test itself is known to be conservative \citep{fagerland2013mcnemar}, so ``switch to exact'' trades
anti-conservative bias for evolution throughput rather than restoring a true
nominal level. The design space is a dial --- uncorrected, corrected, mid-p,
exact --- and each position buys a different error profile. The direction is
also robust to the threshold: sweeping $\alpha$ across
$\{0.01, 0.025, 0.05, 0.10\}$ yields 215, 224, 231, 233 anti-conservative
flips. And the corrected variant overshoots where the audited one erred: at
the band's worst cell ($b{=}18$, $c{=}8$) it returns $0.077556$ against the
exact $0.075519$. None of this
machinery is new; the contribution is the audit trail that forces a gate to
say which dial position it occupies.

Three worked examples from the discontinuity band. The largest
understatement among the 231 flips --- the sharpest, measured as the ratio
of exact to code $p$ at a flip cell --- is $1.525$ at $b{=}19$, $c{=}8$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rrrrrl}
\toprule
$b$ & $c$ & $n$ & code $p$ & exact $p$ & exact/code \\
\midrule
18 & 8 & 26 & 0.049860 & 0.075519 & $1.51\times$ \\
19 & 8 & 27 & 0.034264 & 0.052239 & $1.52\times$ \\
22 & 10 & 32 & 0.033895 & 0.050102 & $1.48\times$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent At $b{=}18$, $c{=}8$ ($n{=}26$) the code returns $p=0.049860$ ---
barely under the threshold, a promotion --- where the exact test returns
$p=0.075519$, comfortably above it. The reference is not home-made:
\code{scipy.stats.binomtest(8, 26, 0.5).pvalue} returns
0.07551869750022888, and the gate returns 0.049860.

The flips are a threshold-band phenomenon, not a diffuse smear: every one of
the 231 has exact $p$ in $(0.050005,\,0.075519]$, and 218 of them in
$(0.05,\,0.06]$. The code's error never reaches two cells' width in $b-c$
anywhere that matters --- it only has to reach one threshold. The 0.42\%
headline is correspondingly a bound-dependent summary, not an invariant: at
$n\le200$ the same function flips 1.02\% of its promotions.

The band's width says \emph{where} the gate errs; the loop says \emph{why it
matters}. The two error columns are not symmetric in consequence. A false
rejection discards one candidate --- an opportunity cost paid once. A false
promotion writes the promoted variant into the ledger as an improvement, and
the next generation starts from it: the variant is re-queried, forked, and
serves as the comparison baseline, so the error is inherited by everything
downstream. The compounding arithmetic is a scenario, not a measurement, and we label its
two inputs. If each of $145$ decisions independently carried the gate's nominal
one-sided budget $q=0.025$, at least one false promotion would occur with
probability $1-(1-0.025)^{145}=0.9746$; that is a property of any $\alpha=0.05$
gate run $145$ times. If instead each decision carried the defect's cell-level
flip proportion $231/55{,}469=0.42\%$, the same arithmetic gives $0.454$.
Neither number is an estimate of this gate's field risk: the $145$ are
modifications in the published Darwin Gödel Machine logs, not this gate's
decisions, and the $0.42\%$ is a proportion over enumerated promotion cells,
not a probability under the distribution of real candidates. What the
enumeration does establish is that $0.42\%$ of the cells this gate would
promote are decided the other way by the exact reference; how that translates
into ledger risk depends on a candidate distribution this paper does not have. A 0.42\% decision-level flip rate is easy
to dismiss as a rounding concern; the compounding arithmetic is why it is not. The pattern --- flat accounting
that hides where the cost actually lands --- is the same one that makes
per-token cost accounting matter in agent loops; acceptance gates deserve
the same treatment.

The switch itself is worth pausing on. For $n \le 25$ the function runs its
exact branch, and that branch is transcription-verified against the
reference; the exhaustive dynamic audit of \S\ref{sec:dynamic} scans only
$n \ge 26$, so the exact branch's correctness on its own domain rests on
transcription and spot checks, not on the same cell-by-cell recomputation we
demand of the approximation. The boundary is invisible at the call site ---
the caller passes a pair of win counts and receives a number that changes
character halfway through its domain.

\section{F-02: one file, or a pattern?}\label{sec:f02}

Is the defect one file or a family trait? \code{acceptance\_gate\_audit.py}
walks the workspace tree, finds every function whose name looks like a paired
binomial test, and classifies each body \emph{statically} --- exact tokens
versus asymptotic tokens --- before any dynamic check. The classifier is
name-gated, and that is a boundary, not an implementation detail: a body that
reaches an approximation through a helper it does not recognise, or hides one
under an innocuous name, is invisible to it; conversely a correct exact test
named \code{mcnemar\_fast} would never be flagged. Its recall is a property
of the workspace's naming conventions, the 48 unclassifiable helpers below
are a standing blind spot, and the dynamic audits of \S\ref{sec:dynamic}
mitigate --- they do not prove absence.

Over the whole workspace (inventory frozen at
\code{data/f02\_gate\_inventory.json}; see \S\ref{app:repro} for a
re-run delta of $+3$ caused by our own artifact archive):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrl}
\toprule
Class & Count & Contents \\
\midrule
exact-only & 60 & every \code{mcnemar\_scipy}, every \code{mcnemar\_two\_sided} \\
unknown & 48 & test helpers, power/$n$/OR calculators, \code{chi2} wrappers \\
approx-only & 7 & all seven are \code{mcnemar\_chi2} (Yates-corrected chi-square) \\
hybrid & 3 & \textbf{all three are named \code{mcnemar\_exact}} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The three hybrids (errors at the $n\le200$ dynamic bound):

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{lrlrl}

Path & line & flips ($n\le200$) & max abs err & verdict \\

The honest headline is \emph{not} ``the workspace's gates are broken.'' Sixty
implementations are exact-only, including every \code{mcnemar\_scipy} and
every \code{mcnemar\_two\_sided} in the \code{gsm8k-self-evolve} and
\code{paper\_workflow\_kb} trees, and \code{paper\_joint\_mem\_forget\_formal}'s
own \code{mcnemar\_exact}. The defect is localised to two copies of one
function in \code{evo\_agent} --- and the two files are genuinely distinct
(\code{evo\_agent/evolve.py}: 6{,}486\,B, mtime 2026-09-13 --- pre-repair
metadata; the live copy was repaired on 2026-10-17, see \S\ref{sec:limits};
\code{evo\_agent/frozen\_v6/evolve.py}: 3{,}421\,B, mtime 2026-09-10), not a
symlink, so two copies exist; which was copied from which is not recorded, and
we do not claim a direction. A name/body disagreement is exactly what the
audit is built to surface, and it surfaced exactly three, two of which are
real. (The third is the verbatim transcription the audit itself carries; the
two \code{reproduces evolve.py} assertions in its selftest exist to catch that
transcription drifting from its source.)

The 48 ``unknown'' rows are now resolved as well, and none hides an
approximation. Twelve of them are $(b,c)$-callable and were audited
dynamically at the $n\le200$ bound: five two-parameter copies
(\code{mcnemar\_two\_sided} / \code{mcnemar\_exact} across
\code{ai-researcher} and both trees of
\code{paper\_joint\_mem\_\allowbreak forget\_formal})
come back exact with zero decision flips and max abs error at most
$9.3\times10^{-14}$, and seven \code{exact\_mcnemar} copies across seven
\code{stats.py} files --- whose $(b,c,\text{alternative})$ signature still
audits cleanly because the third parameter defaults to
\code{"two-sided"} --- come back exact with zero flips and max abs error
$1.4\times10^{-13}$. The remaining 36 are not p-value gates at all: 21
power/$n$/OR calculators whose inputs are effect sizes and target power,
12 test or selftest assertions, and 3 nested closures or recomputers that
delegate to an exact implementation. The resolution also runs the
classifier's reverse blind spot from the inside: the exact
$2\times\min$-tail pattern recurs across seven additional copies that the
name-gated pass left unclassified, so ``unknown'' turned out to contain
more exact code, not less.

Is this failure a pattern or an anecdote? A public-code presence survey
(methods, per-source raw responses, and all fetched bodies are archived
with the reproduction materials) searched Sourcegraph and grep.app for
\code{mcnemar} and found 17{,}335 line matches across 5{,}746 files on
Sourcegraph, of which 1{,}020 across 277 files name
\code{mcnemar\_exact} itself. Seventy-nine files were fetched, 78 of which
yielded 81 non-test implementation definitions classified at function-body
level; the seventy-ninth was fetched only as a delegate target and contributes
no definition. The 78 are the files whose search hit exposed a body that could
be fetched, listed one by one in
\code{files-manifest-exact-definers.json} (which also records the seventy-ninth,
a delegate target outside the 277-file population); they are a convenience
sample of the 277 candidates rather than a random draw, so the count below is a
within-sample count and not a prevalence estimate. The screening record and
every fetched body are archived with the reproduction
materials. Sixty-eight definitions carry \emph{exact} in the name: 61 contain
a direct exact implementation (combinatorial tail sums,
\code{binomtest}, fraction or log-space arithmetic), four delegate
honestly to exact helpers, two wrap exact routines, one formats without
computing. That first criterion, applied to a body read alone, cleared all
68, because it asked only whether an exact formula was present. On that
reading, the approximation bodies the survey did flag sat under names that
make no exact promise (\code{mcnemar\_normal}, \code{mcnemar\_chi2\_cc},
\code{mcnemar\_asymptotic}), and one evaluation harness co-reports exact and
asymptotic $p$-values under separately named functions in the same file. The
reading was blind to a sample-size switch inside an otherwise exact body.

\paragraph{But one of the 68 does break its name, and our first criterion
missed it.} Re-adjudicating the same 68 definitions under a criterion that
looks for a \emph{sample-size switch} rather than a \emph{formula} returns
one counterexample. \code{Gurp-S/XEYO}\allowbreak/\allowbreak
\code{python/evals/changedetect/stats.py} defines
\code{mcnemar\_exact(b, c)}, sets \code{\_EXACT\_MAX\_N = 500}, and at
\code{stats.py:67--68} returns \code{mcnemar\_normal(b, c)} whenever
$n = b+c > 500$ --- the continuity-corrected normal approximation, i.e. the
same defect class as the F-01 target, in a public repository, under a name
that promises exactness. The original classification recorded this function
as \code{exact} because its small-$n$ body is exactly a
\code{math.comb}-based tail sum. \textbf{The first criterion was wrong, not
the code}: it asked ``is there an exact formula here'', which this file
answers yes to, instead of ``does the function return the exact tail for
every input it accepts'', which it does not.

That correction changes the headline from \emph{absence} to a
\emph{counterexample count}, and it also sharpens the finding in a way the
absence claim could not. The
counterexample is the same defect as the one this paper audits --- an
\code{exact}-named gate that silently degrades past a threshold ---
discovered in independent public code by a criterion that only became visible
once the F-01 target taught us what to look for. So we state the corrected
result: \textbf{1 of 68 exact-named implementations in a 78-file public
sample silently substitutes a normal approximation above a sample-size
threshold; the remaining 67 were not observed to do so.} A second file,
\code{NVIDIA/SkillEvaluator}, is the instructive contrast: it computes the
tail in exact rational arithmetic (\code{Fraction}, \code{collector.py:4587})
with no switch at all, and handles float underflow by returning \code{None}
plus a separate ``minimum attainable $p$'' instead of degrading the
$p$-value --- the same numeric pressure, handled by disclosure rather than
substitution.

\paragraph{What the corrected count does and does not support.} It is not a
prevalence estimate. The source population (1{,}020 line matches across 277
files naming \code{mcnemar\_exact}) is not a frame from which 78 files were
drawn at random; no confidence interval is computed; two files were
unreachable; and the 68 aggregate four categories of unequal evidentiary
strength (61 body reads, 4 delegate-verified, 2 wrappers, 1 that computes no
$p$-value and therefore cannot fail the test it is counted in). That last
category leaves an effective computational denominator of 67, although we keep
``1 of 68'' so the denominator remains tied to the name-selection criterion.
A presence claim survives all of this --- the defect exists in public code,
independently of this workspace --- and a rarity or rate claim does not. The
counterexample also confirms the contract violation, not the direction result:
its fallback is the continuity-corrected normal, which \S\ref{sec:f01} shows
runs conservative, so we do not claim the XEYO instance favors promotion. The
qualitative reading we do stand behind is the gradient: the name is usually
honest, which is exactly why a name that is not escapes notice.

\noindent\textbf{A further axis this survey does not measure.} The correction
from $0$ to $1$ out of 68 is a count on the exactness axis only. Sidedness is
not instrumented
(\S\ref{sec:conclusion}, last row): a function correctly named for exactness
can still hand a one-sided $p$-value to a caller expecting two-sided. And the
$n\le200$ dynamic bound used for the \emph{workspace} targets does not apply
here at all --- these 68 were read statically, and the counterexample above
is precisely a case where a static read at small $n$ is silent about the
large-$n$ branch. We report the count as observed within this convenience
sample, not as a rate among \code{exact}-named functions at large.

The last row's error deserves its own paragraph, because it is not the
asymptotic gap the ``asymptotic'' label predicts. The audited implementation
computes $\chi^2 = (|b-c|-1)^2/(b+c)$ \emph{without clamping the correction
at ties}: when $b = c$ the term evaluates to $1/n$ instead of $0$, and the
function returns $p = 0.8445$ where the correct value is $1.0$ --- an error

region: its 88 tie cells contribute that $0.155481$ artifact, while away
from ties the same continuity-corrected form errs by at most $0.002053$ (at

hold --- but it is a second, independent instance of this paper's subject: a
formula whose boundary case silently disagrees with its reference, in the
very implementation that represents the ``fixed'' end of the dial in
\S\ref{sec:f01}. Its five decision flips (nine at $n\le500$) are all
conservative-direction.

\code{retrieval-demo/bline/w2\_harness.py} imports a sibling module
(\code{w1\_hybrid}) that is not on \code{sys.path}. The tool \textbf{refuses
and exits} with a non-zero status ($2$, \code{FAIL-CLOSED}) rather than
reporting a clean result. Note the scope of this guarantee: it covers
\emph{execution} failure --- a target that cannot be imported or called, an
empty inventory, a vacuous $n_{\max}<26$ region. It does not cover
\emph{verdict} failure; see \S\ref{sec:semantics}.

\section{Two defects in our own tools}\label{sec:selfaudit}

Both defects are the same class of error the audit hunts --- a discrepancy
between what a check claims and what it does --- which is why they are
reported as first-class results.

\paragraph{1. Display rounding conflated with approximation.}
\code{retrieval-demo/bline/w3\_harness.py} computes the exact tail correctly
and then rounds the output to 4 decimals, giving $5\times10^{-5}$ of benign
error. The first version of our exactness check used a $10^{-12}$ tolerance
and would have reported that \emph{correct} exact test as non-exact. The fix
splits the question in two: \code{decision\_flips} (the decision-relevant
question: does the verdict change?) and \code{max\_abs\_error} (reported
separately), with an explicit \code{EXACT\_TOL = 1e-4} and an
\code{output\_rounded} flag. The tolerance is not arbitrary: $1\times10^{-4}$
is twice the largest benign error a 4-decimal display can introduce
($5\times10^{-5}$), and it sits more than three orders of magnitude below the
errors the approximations actually produce: $0.149446$ for mid-p, $0.152610$
for the audited target, and $0.1555$ for the continuity-corrected copy at
$(13,13,26)$, of which at most $0.0021$ is asymptotic gap and the rest is the
tie artifact.

\paragraph{2. An injection that detected nothing, silently.}
The \code{acceptance\_gate\_audit.py} gate's \code{-{}-inject no-flips} branch
body was \code{pass}: the
injection changed nothing, the gate exited 0 --- meaning ``not detected'' ---
and printed nothing about it. A self-test that passes while its fault
injection does nothing is worse than no self-test. It was replaced with
\code{skip-renamed} (an auditor that walks past every function named
\code{mcnemar\_exact}), which the ``scan locates the F-01 target'' invariant
does catch. Two further guards came out of the same pass: a
\code{most\_severe\_flips[0]} index on an empty list that crashed two
injections instead of failing them, and a band assertion corrected because the
most severe flip sits at $n{=}32$, not at the switch itself.

A third injection is kept permanently in the suite: \code{same-reference},
where the reference \emph{is} the audited function. An audit whose oracle is
its target can never find anything, so the gate must fail if that
configuration ever occurs.

Read as a set, the injections are a control system for the auditor itself,
and it is worth naming the mapping explicitly:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lp{7.2cm}}
\toprule
Injection & Control it provides \\
\midrule
\code{skip-renamed} & exemption-resistance: an auditor that walks past the
F-01 target must not look clean \\
\code{same-reference} & circularity: an oracle equal to its target can never
detect anything \\
\code{conservative-bias} & direction-sensitivity: the pipeline must react to
flips in \emph{either} direction \\
\code{empty-scan} & degenerate input must fail closed, not report a clean
bill \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent One retired injection taught its own lesson: in
\code{acceptance\_gate\_audit.py}, the original \code{no-flips} branch body was
\code{pass} --- a named control that controlled nothing --- and it survived
until a human read the outputs (defect 2 above). (\code{mcnemar\_audit.py}
still carries a working \code{no-flips} injection, which clears the flip list
and is caught by the expected-flip-count assertion.) A control that cannot fail
is decoration.

\section{What the exit code means}\label{sec:semantics}

One property of our own instruments needs stating before \S\ref{sec:gates},
because it is the exact defect this paper is about applied to the auditor
itself. Read the two CLI branches of
\code{acceptance\_gate\_audit.py} (lines 304--316 as pinned by the
\code{sha256} prefix in \S\ref{sec:gates}):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{--audit} calls \code{dynamic\_audit}, returns $2$ if the result
  is \code{None}, otherwise prints the JSON --- \textbf{including}
  \code{"is\_exact": false} and \code{"decision\_flips": 84} --- and
  \textbf{returns $0$ unconditionally}.
  \item \code{--selftest --inject} is the branch that behaves as a gate: it
  returns $1$ when an injected violation is detected and $0$ when the
  injection is \emph{not} detected, printing
  \code{*** NOT DETECTED --- GATE IS DECORATIVE ***}.
\end{itemize}

So the tool has two distinct exit vocabularies, and they are not the same
vocabulary. On the \code{--audit} path, exit $0$ means \emph{the audit ran},
not \emph{the target passed}. Re-measured on 2026-10-25: auditing
\code{../evo\_agent/frozen\_v6/evolve.py:mcnemar\_exact} returns exit $0$ with
\code{is\_exact: false}, 84 decision flips over 10{,}019 cells and
\code{max\_abs\_error: 0.15261}; auditing
\code{../paper\_workflow\_kb/scripts/stats.py:mcnemar\_chi2} returns exit $0$
with 5 flips and \code{max\_abs\_error: 0.155481}. Both are \emph{failures of
the audited artefact} reported at exit $0$.

We therefore describe \code{--audit} and \code{--workspace-scan} as
\textbf{reporters}, not admission gates: they fail closed on execution
errors (unimportable target, empty inventory, vacuous region --- all verified
at exit $2$), and they record verdicts in JSON for a consumer to read. The
phrase ``fail-closed gate'' in \S\ref{sec:gates} applies to the selftest
injection suite only. The \code{sha256}-pinned behaviour is unchanged from
what produced the results in this paper; we are correcting the description,
not the record.

\noindent\textbf{Why we did not simply fix it.} Making \code{--audit} exit
non-zero on \code{is\_exact: false} would be a three-line change, and it is
tempting because it would let the tool's name keep its promise. We declined
for two reasons. First, \code{is\_exact: false} is the \emph{expected} result
for every target this audit was built to find --- the F-01 target itself ---
so a non-zero exit on discovery would make the reporter unusable inside the
very pipeline that consumes it; discovery and admission are different
contracts and conflating them is how a gate acquires a name it cannot honour.
Second, changing the exit semantics now would silently reinterpret the
2026-10-17 run recorded in App.~\ref{app:repro} as having ``passed'' under a
stricter contract, which it did not. The honest repair is a separate,
explicitly-named admission mode with its own negative and positive tests;
that is future work, and it is listed as such in \S\ref{sec:limits}.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

None of the statistics here is new, and the paper's debts are specific.
McNemar's test became the standard instrument for paired classifier
comparison through \citep{dietterich1998approximate}. The exact conditional
test this audit treats as its reference is itself known to be conservative,
with mid-p and corrected asymptotic variants recommended in its place
\citep{fagerland2013mcnemar} --- which is why \S\ref{sec:f01} presents a dial
rather than a repair. The wider discipline of testing when comparing learned
systems --- multiplicity, test-set reuse, significance inflation --- is
surveyed in \citep{dror2018hitchhiker}; at the systems layer, flaky and
retried tests in CI pipelines present the same accept-on-noise hazard
\citep{luo2016flaky}. Acceptance gates for learning agents are the subject of
the safe-reinforcement-learning literature \citep{garcia2015safe}. The
optional-stopping hazard of repeated testing is classical
\citep{wald1945sequential}, and the racing/early-stopping acceptance
machinery of algorithm configuration and HPO --- F-Race/irace
\citep{birattari2016irace}, Hyperband \citep{li2017hyperband}, ASHA
\citep{jamieson2016asha} --- makes precisely the trade this paper audits:
promote early on partial evidence. The contract lens on names is Meyer's
design-by-contract \citep{meyer1992contract}, imported here from module
interfaces to statistical claims. At the agent layer, skill-library
self-improvement \citep{wang2023voyager}, self-reflection loops
\citep{shinn2023reflexion}, and co-evolution traps \citep{feng2026cods} are the
systems that would consume such gates; catalogs of self-modification
decisions \citep{he2026failure} are the loops that would generate their
candidates; and LLM-as-judge reliability \citep{zheng2023judging} bounds the
label validity any such audit must assume. The contribution is none of these
instruments; it is the audit trail that binds one named gate to its measured
behaviour --- and the demonstration that both a one-line repair and a
boundary case can hide behind a correct-sounding name.

\section{What this does not establish}\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The audited system's promotion history has been rejudged ---
  read-only, and the harm remains potential.} The fourteen entries of the
  audited system's evolution ledger
  (\code{evo\_agent/EVOLUTION\_LEDGER.md}, excluding the smoke-record block
  that the ledger itself marks as not counted toward gate statistics) were
  re-evaluated against
  the exact reference: all fourteen recorded verdicts agree (five promotions,
  nine holds; every entry sits at $n\le12$, inside the exact branch), and the
  recorded $p$-values match the reference to their display rounding
  ($0.0078$ vs.\ $0.0078125$, $0.0156$ vs.\ $0.015625$). This was an analysis
  we ran, not a repair we made: whether to re-derive past promotions is the
  system owner's call, and repairing the function would still invalidate the
  ledger's provenance.
  \emph{Repair record (owner-authorized, recorded after the audited
  snapshot):} after this draft entered internal review, the owner elected to repair the
  \emph{live} gate \code{evo\_agent/evolve.py} (exact branch extended to all
  $n$; sha256 \code{32ab7425...} $\to$ \code{bb2d3d2b...}); the frozen
  snapshot \code{frozen\_v6/evolve.py} is deliberately left unrepaired --- it
  is the audited object of this paper and the reproduction anchor. The
  post-repair dynamic audit of the live gate reads 0 decision flips, max abs
  error $2.0\times10^{-15}$ at $n\le200$ and $2.7\times10^{-15}$ at $n\le500$
  (float noise), and
  the ledger rejudgment above is unchanged (14/14). Every number in this paper
  refers to the archived pre-repair sources
  (App.~\ref{app:repro}).
  The loop-level exposure is also not zero in general: a
  gate consulted once per candidate accumulates threshold-adjacent risk, and
  at one-sided $\alpha{=}0.025$, 145 independent decisions sitting exactly at
  the band edge would carry a $1-(1-0.025)^{145} \approx 97.5\%$ chance of at
  least one false promotion --- an illustrative worst case, not a property of
  this ledger, whose entries sit far from the band (at the defect's measured
  flip rate the same arithmetic gives $\approx 45\%$, \S\ref{sec:f01}).
  \item \textbf{Label validity is upstream of this paper.} The wins that feed
  $b$ and $c$ are judged by regex matchers (\code{judge\_strict},
  \code{judge\_content} in the audited file); if a judge mislabels a task
  outcome, the counts are wrong before any test runs. The audit takes the
  recorded labels as given and does not measure the judges' agreement with
  ground truth \citep{zheng2023judging}.
  \item \textbf{The static classifier reads names and bodies.} As stated in
  \S\ref{sec:f02}: recall is name-gated, helpers are a blind spot, and the
  dynamic audits are a mitigation, not a proof of absence.
  \item \textbf{The audit pins the target by value, and the value pins
  protect transcription only.} \code{mcnemar\_audit.py} transcribes the
  audited function; its two \code{reproduces evolve.py} selftest assertions
  compare that transcription against \emph{hardcoded} reference pairs
  ($p(18,8)=0.049860$, $p(19,8)=0.034264$, tolerance $5\times10^{-7}$;
  \code{mcnemar\_audit.py:200--203}) --- they never read the audited file, so
  they would not notice \code{evo\_agent} drifting. The live guard is the
  dynamic audit of \S\ref{sec:dynamic}, which imports the actual file; the
  honest statement is that between transcription checks and ad-hoc dynamic
  runs there is no standing watch.
  \item \textbf{$n \le 500$ is a scan bound, not a natural limit.} The flip
  count grows with the bound: 84 flips at $n\le200$, 231 at $n\le500$ ---
  and the flip \emph{rate} moves too (1.02\% to 0.42\% of promotions), so
  single-number headlines are bound-relative in two ways.
  \item \textbf{One workspace, one defect body.} All counts are from a single
  research workspace's trees; the classifier's recall elsewhere is
  unmeasured, and a finding this localised (two copies of one function)
  licenses no prevalence claim about other codebases.
  \item \textbf{Neither tool is an admission gate, and no admission gate is
  built here.} As shown in \S\ref{sec:semantics}, \code{--audit} and
  \code{--workspace-scan} exit $0$ on a target they have just declared
  non-exact; only the selftest injection suite exits non-zero on a violation.
  Nothing in this paper blocks a promotion automatically --- the two self-audit
  defects of \S\ref{sec:selfaudit} were caught by a human reading outputs, not
  by a gate rejecting a build, and the \code{no-flips} injection survived
  precisely because exit $0$ was indistinguishable from success. A genuine
  admission mode would need a named contract, a non-zero exit on
  \code{is\_exact: false}, and its own negative test (a target that must be
  rejected) and positive test (a target that must pass); we have designed none
  of those, so the claim is not made.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

The intended reader is the author of the next acceptance rule, and the
reviewer of the next self-evolution paper. The audit's recipe is four moves,
each executable in this workspace:

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{p{2.2cm}p{3.7cm}p{3.3cm}p{3.1cm}}
\toprule
Name / artifact & Executable property it claims & Fail-closed gate & Drift control \\
\midrule
\code{...\_exact} & flips $=0$, max abs err $\le 10^{-4}$ over the decision region & \code{--audit} imports and executes and prints \code{is\_exact}; the non-zero exit is enforced by the selftest injection \code{dynamic-clean}, not by \code{--audit} itself & dynamic re-run against the live file \\
transcription & reproduces the audited source & selftest value pins (catch hand-edits only) & dynamic audit reads the file \\
classifier claims & static tokens + dynamic confirmation & \code{--workspace-scan} (reporter) + \code{--selftest} injections (gate) & frozen inventory with delta note \\
sidedness / $\alpha$ axis & --- & \emph{not yet instrumented}: the name determines more contract axes than this audit tests & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent A name suffix is a claim; a claim becomes an executable property
(region recomputation against an independent reference); the property is
\textbf{reported} by a tool that fails closed on execution error and records
its verdict where a reader can see it; and drift is controlled by importing
the live file rather than trusting a transcription. We stop short of calling
the last step a gate, for the reason given in \S\ref{sec:semantics}: a check
that a human must read is a leverage point, not an admission rule. What the
recipe found here: a gate whose name promised exactness, whose body delivered
an uncorrected approximation, and whose every decision error --- relative to
the level-matched reference --- favored the action the gate exists to permit;
plus a continuity correction in the ``fixed'' variant that silently errs at
ties, and a display-tolerance trap in our own first instrument. What the
recipe cannot deliver is recall beyond recognisable names, prevalence beyond
one workspace, or the repair decision, which belongs to the system's owner.
